# Cam-ready deliverable patterns

Skeletons and rules that came directly from Cam's feedback. Start from these instead of inventing structure. Nine patterns: six deliverable shapes, the build pipeline behind them, the layout and copy rules he enforces on every page, and the rules for working with him directly.

Sources: the 2026-08-20 and 2026-09-03 1:1s, the Enviro Pro and Inforcer client emails, the reputation engine deck review, and the Big 5 eavestrough and vinyl siding page reviews.

**How to use this file.** Find the pattern that matches what you were asked for and build to it. If nothing matches, patterns 7 and 8 still apply, because almost everything eventually becomes a page or a doc someone has to read.

---

## 1. Client activity report (reputation engine model)

The loop-closing shape Cam specified, in his numbers:

> "Right now we need a report that calls out 21 topic opportunities, 17 responses, three positive interactions, and 30 days later, 15 of those 17 responses are still live."

Structure:

1. **Headline numbers**: opportunities identified > actioned > secured > still live at 30 days. Business-owner KPIs only (conversation opportunities, brand references, local sentiment). No technical metrics.
2. **Two or three concrete examples with links.** The "money in the bank" Patriot comment, not a screenshot wall.
3. **Topic frequency insight**: "conversations about roofing come up in your regional subreddits X times a week." First-party data nobody else has. It makes the agency look dialed in and it seeds future statistical content.
4. **What happens next.**

Design: clean, labeled, nothing Matrix. Color paired with labels, never carrying meaning alone (Cam is colorblind).

## 2. Client recap email (Enviro Pro model)

1. **Acknowledge their state**, one personal-stake line. Cam opens with his own 2012 carpet-cleaning story and the $3k/month that was cutting deep before it worked.
2. **Where we are**: momentum with honest gaps, real ranking positions, no rounding up. "We're not yet cracking the front page."
3. **The plan**: what changes this month, in what order, and why.
4. **Performance targets**: dated, conservative, only what can be shown. "Mid-September: AI describes you correctly."
5. **Your homework**: specific asks of the client (reviews, before/afters with a few bullets each, pricing ranges), because the trust signals the agency cannot generate belong to them.
6. **Receipts where possible**: the clean-ChatGPT test with a shareable link.

Tone: personal stake first, structure second, honest about what is unfinished or AI-generated, always an invitation rather than a defense.

## 3. Sales narrative (slide deck or landing page)

Order that survived Cam's review of the reputation engine deck:

1. **Value pillars, benefit-phrased.** Watches everywhere that matters; identifies opportunities in real time; not an ad, they are asking for help; you know what to do next. Cam killed "scores what it finds" and "one private link" as pillars: features, not benefits.
2. **The lead moment**, in directive-marketing framing. Someone typing "window pane repair Southeast Calgary" is asking for a solution; a billboard is hoping. "You're not buying hope, you are buying access to the lead stream."
3. **Exclusivity.** Only you know this conversation is happening.
4. **How it works**, one plain slide.
5. **Safety, on its own slide**, never mixed with value. Plain statements: no data stored, no laws violated, a human reviews before anything is acted on. Say "there is no data," not "your data is kept private," which only makes a business owner wonder what data you have.
6. **How you know it's working**: the three KPIs, then the report from pattern 1.

Landing-page version of the same energy, Cam's sketch: "Have an invested neighbor in every community in your city. Know when they need help, be the first to respond."

## 4. Content outline assignment (the phase-one deliverable)

When Cam asks for a content outline, he wants exactly this and nothing more:

- Sections of the page, in order.
- Key points each section must cover.
- No wireframe, no design, no copy.

Prerequisite thinking he expects behind it: who the ICP is, what keeps them up at night, what they have already tried (are we their first SEO firm or their tenth), what objections have to die on this page.

His research chain for finding the content mix: play the buyer in a clean ChatGPT conversation > note what it asks and suggests > pull Semrush keyword and question data > AlsoAsked / People Also Ask expansion > group into themes > the page must answer those themes. Then apply CEOV per concern and the 4 Cs to the whole.

**Section order is a data decision, not a taste decision.** He pulls keyword data before the outline specifically "to make sure that when we orient the homepage, we're orienting it in the appropriate order of priority." On Remedy Roofworks, Spokane's keyword data beat Coeur d'Alene decisively, so the homepage tilted to Spokane.

Scope fences are the assignment. When he says phase one is the outline and he does not want a wireframe, staying inside that fence is what he is grading.

## 5. Page content standard

A service page is complete when all of the following are true. The first five came out of the Big 5 eavestrough build; the last four came out of the 2026-09-03 vinyl siding review.

- **Every service variant is named**, with the reason to choose each. Installation, repair, replacement. 5" vs 6" eavestrough. Standard vs insulated vinyl. Hardie panel vs Hardie plank lap. Downspouts and why placement matters. If it is not on the page, neither humans nor AI know you do it.
- **Objections are answered in place.** The multi-generation family business block exists to overcome "will you still be here in a year."
- **"What your quote covers" appears where the reader starts deciding.**
- **Claims that differ from the market carry proof.** AI treats a proven contrarian claim as novel and an unproven one as suspicious.
- **The buyer can visualize the result on their own home**: colors, materials, textures, before and afters.
- **There is somewhere to see the work.** A before/after gallery or an install walkthrough. Cam's catch on the vinyl page: "Why don't you show me some examples of siding you installed? Where would I go to see that right now?" When the media does not exist, prescribe it. "It's actually the single most important thing that you can do."
- **The cross-sell is clear but contained.** Name the honest reason to choose the other product, in the smallest visual footprint that still reads. A large Hardie block on a vinyl page throws the whole page off.
- **The location block is complete.** Full NAP (business name, shop address, phone), the map embed, and links out to the individual location pages.
- **The layout was chosen for this product**, not inherited from the last page. See pattern 7.

## 6. Page build pipeline (research to Divi)

The sequence Cam ran end to end on 2026-09-03. Steps A through E are research and live in one AI chat; F through L are the build.

### A. Set up

Three tabs: Claude, Search Console, Semrush.

### B. Gather context

- Sales/intro call and kickoff call transcripts.
- The client's current page saved as HTML (carries colors and fonts).
- Logo and hero-grade photography. Contractor trucks are reliably good.
- Public Google Business Profile info.

### C. Brief the research chat

Tell it plainly: we are redeveloping this client's website, we are kicking off an AEO/SEO strategy, here are the call transcripts, the primary task is a homepage wireframe. Then ask what else it needs to know, and answer its questions with decisions rather than deferrals:

- **Sitemap**: assume the same as the current site unless told otherwise.
- **Assets**: public GBP info now, placeholders everywhere else.
- **Reference build**: new wireframe from scratch, do not borrow heavily from anything existing.
- **Service weighting**: no single service gets outsized importance just because the client mentioned it a lot.
- **Data access**: state which sources you can pull and which you cannot. Semrush pulled manually, not via MCP. No Search Console yet.

### D. Pull the data

**Established client, full audit set**: Search Console for the specific pages, Search Console for the whole site, query reports; Semrush for the whole site, that specific URL, the domain overview, and two or three competitors. In Cam's words: "Basically what I am doing is a quasi audit in the sense of, where is this page falling down based on the data, what's happening. Then the prescription, the remedy to fix it, is based on all of that data."

**New client with no history**:
1. Semrush > Domain Overview > organic traffic > six-month view > export the table. That is the only report worth handing over from the domain side.
2. Ask Claude for **150 keywords** relevant to the services, in one bulk copy-paste block.
3. Semrush > Keyword Strategy Builder > create a regular list with your own keywords > name it for the client > paste the 150 > let the data populate > scroll to the bottom to confirm it finished. Not every keyword returns data; "some keywords are just shit keywords."
4. Export. XLSX or CSV, does not matter.

Send both exports on their own turn with "this is all the data that I can pull." Do not staple a large attachment to a large ask; it eats time while the model digests.

### E. Sanity check before you commit

Ask for a rough outline of every section on the page plus a one-line summary of the content in each, explicitly including trust bars, testimonials, and the rest of the page furniture. Cam's framing: "This is a version of a sanity check. We're just making sure that it's going to do what we think it should."

Confirm three things: every real service is present, including ones raised on calls but missing from the current site; nothing has outsized weight; the order reflects keyword priority.

### F. Design brief

Ask for a Claude design brief. **The brief is the work.** Once it exists, most build prompts are literally "see attached." Cam: "How many of my prompts in Claude Design are 'see attached'? Probably about 75% of them. It's the whole reason I do the brief."

### G. Build the wireframe

New Claude Design instance. Attach the brief, the logo, and the saved site HTML. Then state explicitly, because neither survives a regeneration:

- use the client's colors from the attached HTML
- render the mobile and tablet views alongside desktop

Cam on why: "If you didn't give it the explicit instruction, there's a good chance it doesn't carry forward."

### H. Review section by section, out loud, into the tool

Feed verdicts straight back as prompts. Ask for two or three variants when a section is not landing, and pick on visual distinctness. Screenshot outside references and name the takeaway: "I like the way the pictures are presented, it's a little bit different, it feels quirky. Could I get something like that for this section?"

### I. Validate against the data

Export the revised page and take it back to the research chat: **does this revised content flow meet the goals for AEO and SEO?** Cam is specifically listening for a cannibalization warning against sibling pages. If it comes back broadly fine, move on.

### J. Massage the copy

Three moves in order: **format it, make it sound less dorky, make it more specific.** Do it directly in the design file. If the content chops are not there, route it to a copywriter rather than ship it. Cam will pick apart a wrong "there" every time.

### K. Optimize tablet and mobile

### L. Export a Divi JSON, import, clean up during the build

The JSON is a starting point, not a finished page. Expect to fix spacing, containers, and module choices while building.

**Benchmark**: a settled wireframe becomes a built page in roughly one to one and a half hours. Longer than that means the wireframe was not finished.

**Sharing work in progress**: a Claude workspace link only resolves for people inside that workspace. Publish as an artifact for anyone outside it. When the reviewer needs to edit rather than look, send the HTML file instead. "In a lot of ways, sending the HTML file is better in this context."

## 7. Layout and hierarchy patterns

Cam's background is content production, which is why his page feedback is directorial before it is verbal. He is not describing taste; he is describing where your eye goes.

### Direct the eye

"I'm actually directing your eye every step of the way." A working section has a traceable path: bold headline, down, left, bing bing bing. A broken one is where "my eye goes here and then it sort of bounces around, lands here, comes down here." If you cannot trace it in one pass, the section is not cohesive.

**Bold color for what must be read, quiet sub-copy for the rest.** "I don't care if you read that shit or not. I want you to read it, of course. But if you only read this and then this and this, you're getting the point." Bolded phrases are a faster second version of the page. Test: if headlines plus bold text alone do not tell the reader what to do, the emphasis is wrong.

### Never stack two structurally identical sections

A two-column square section directly above another two-column square section reads as "a lot of the same," and image quality does not save it. "The quality of the image matters less here. If there was a section in between these two that broke up the flow, it wouldn't be quite so obvious."

Fix it by changing the section shape between them, not by forcing in a CTA.

### Layout follows the product, not the template

The lesson he named as the inexperience tell:

> "There's no chance the vinyl siding page should have an identical layout to the eavestrough page. Because they're very different products."

One product has colors, patterns, and manufacturers you can show. The other is a coloured piece of aluminum shaped on site. "You took the template and rolled with it, whereas I would have taken the concept and made it more visual, because this is a more visual thing."

Inheriting a working layout is not wrong by itself; he explicitly approved following his wireframe verbatim where the structure genuinely fit. The miss is not noticing that this product wants a different treatment.

**"Concept is good, execution is bad"** is a legitimate and common verdict. Keep the idea, redo the presentation.

### Diagrams

- **One idea per diagram.** The eavestrough water-flow diagram works because it explains exactly one thing, as a one-two-three. "That's a house, there's free stuff." A diagram covering installation types, movement, and materials at once either oversimplifies or becomes unparseable: "I'm not quite sure what I'm looking at."
- **It has to show the actual difference.** Two panels that look identical are a failed comparison. The standard vs insulated vinyl diagram only worked once the insulation layer and air gap were visible.
- **Each item in a list can earn its own small diagram or photo.** On roof cleaning: "specifically I want you to show me an example of what moss on a roof looks like and then have the description."
- **Show the correct method, not just the concept.** He loved the install diagram labelled "nail tight, hung by Big 5" because it demonstrates the right way to do it.

### Show, don't tell

"Show versus tell is amazing." If the claim is that installation quality is the differentiator, show a bad install beside a good one. Do not substantiate a visual claim with another paragraph.

### Comparison sections

- **Never directly under the hero.** "I believe that we should be able to stand on our own merits. And as soon as I'm comparing myself to other people, I'm introducing the probability that people are going to think about other people."
- **Roughly three quarters down, after the pricing conversation.** "Here's the other guy's quote, here's our quote, it's itemized. We just had a conversation really about pricing. I like that a lot more."
- **Cross-sells stay small.** Smallest footprint that still reads clearly.

### Page architecture: sell first, then educate

Cam does not care how long a page is. "I don't give a fuck about how long a page is." Length only becomes a problem when the data says so: if 98% of traffic leaves before the halfway point, the issue is "content alignment relative to user intent," not length.

The shape:

1. **Top half is sales.** Service, service, what we do, buy from us. "Buy, buy, buy, buy, buy."
2. **The pivot.** "Here's what you need to know about eavestrough." Someone who scrolled past several CTAs and is still reading wants information, not another button.
3. **Educate.** Diagrams live down here. "You're halfway down a page that already is quite long about gutters and you're still asking questions. So I'm answering them."
4. **What your quote covers, why us, proof.**
5. **Boilerplate, then FAQ.**

The mental model in his words: "Can you help me? Yes you can, I'm calling you. Hey, you can help me, but I'm still missing some information. I scroll, I keep reading, I scroll, I keep reading."

### Standing rules

- **Not every section needs a CTA.** "I don't need to have a CTA on every single section."
- **Standardized sections are verbatim.** Blocks that appear on every page do not get redesigned per page. "This should be verbatim the section that I built. We're not changing that."
- **Heroes must differ across a portfolio.** The Renovated Northwest hero was rejected purely because it matched every other RoofMax site deployed. "All these guys talk to each other."
- **Full-width copy is hard to read.** Not just headlines, any copy running the full container. Put it in its own container.
- **Two stacked images is normally a no-go zone.** He waived it once because the live build puts the image on the left; the default stands.
- **Compress cards when a section grows.** Breaking a section into its parts should not let it dominate the page.
- **The most prominent photo has to be about the page.** On a vinyl page, the largest photo is vinyl, not the cross-sell.

## 8. Copy patterns

### The 4 Cs

Clear, concise, complete, conversion-focused. No content without a purpose. He names the specific C a page violated: "concise is the C that this has violated. And this. And this."

### Cut it in half

The default instruction on any block of prose, then look again. On a hero: "There's twice as much copy in a hero area than what it should have, and actually maybe even more than that. Get rid of the fucking life story."

### Kill the blob

"This is a big blob. Speaking of blobs, here is another big blob that nobody, including you, is going to read." Break it out, bold the load-bearing phrase, or move it into a toggle. "Whenever you want to have a lot of copy, consider a toggle. Or do you really need to have all that copy?"

### One complete sentence, no qualifier chains

He had Claude strip the pattern out of his own voice: "statement followed by statement, don't like that. No qualifiers." The failure mode is a line that sounds like copy but adds nothing:

| Kill | Ship |
|---|---|
| "New install, repair or full reside. The wall usually decides." | "Vinyl siding works for Calgary's climate when installed correctly." |
| "Cracks at minus 25 when a puck finds them." | "Cracks in cold weather." |
| "Signs your siding is done" | "Signs your vinyl siding is damaged" |
| "A Calgary install" | Say the thing without the keyword wedged in. |
| "Same color run, same visit, one warranty" | "We are a dedicated exterior contractor. We are not a deck contractor that does siding." |
| "The long term option" (for Hardie) | Name the real reasons: fire resistant, impact resistant, long lasting. |

### Write for the person who will not decode you

"You're gonna have a 67-year-old fucking Ukrainian immigrant that goes, what? What does that mean even?"

### Stop being clever

"This copy in general, I find it's like somebody trying really hard to be fucking clever all over the place." Cleverness that costs clarity is the first thing cut, and AI-written copy is the worst offender. "That's the kind of thing that you look at and go, no, die."

### Words and claims he bans

- **"Cheap."** "The word cheap should never be in a headline for a product unless it's actually cheap. Cheap is different than good, valuable, affordable, inexpensive, a lot of things."
- **SEO injection.** Keyword phrasing wedged into a sentence that would never say it. "I never want to read that again."
- **Anything you cannot guarantee.** He killed a same-color-run claim because he could not guarantee it. Replace with something that is always true.
- **Framing your own product as cheap while defending it.** Vinyl should read as "the best that vinyl is gonna be" and still make clear that long lasting and impact resistant means Hardie. Hold both honestly.

### Positive rules

- **Oxford commas.** He wants to see them.
- **Specificity over adjectives.** Numbers he can defend: founded 1984, five and six inch, more than 2 million feet of eavestrough installed. "It wasn't just arbitrary. There's a reason why I had that there."
- **Grammar is load-bearing.** He caught a wrong "there" mid-meeting and openly questioned whether the content chops were there. "I will pick that type of error apart every time."

## 9. Working with Cam

- **He explains once, then expects ownership A to Z.** Take notes the first time.
- **Escalate with "here's the issue and what we tried."** Never a bare status, never a silent stall he discovers by tripping over a broken site.
- **Solve, don't just follow.** He wants instructions executed with judgment applied. "Problem solving is a dying, dying art. I just didn't expect my chief problem solver to be" one of the casualties. Following an instruction into an obviously bad outcome reads as a miss, not obedience.
- **Client work outranks internal work, always.** "That's the least important thing you're doing right now. We can meet when you need to meet, as long as it's not coming at the expense of client work." He will move a 1:1 before he lets client work slip.
- **Write the prescription.** What the client currently has does not constrain what you specify. "My job is to tell them what I need. My job is to write the prescription. The fact that my prescription calls for you to get an hour of exercise a week and you don't currently do that is not my fucking problem. Go buy shoes." Clients expect this: "Scott from Caccia tells us all the time, we pay you to tell us what to do. So do that."
- **Stay inside the scope fence.** When phase one is the outline, bringing a wireframe is not initiative.
- **Flag-only monitoring stays flag-only.** Legal and reputation keywords route to the client contact, never get a response.
- **His cadence, offered not imposed.** "I work a little bit every day right now because I find it's better than not." Aligning on a weekend is framed as a way to get feedback faster, never as an expectation.
- **Heckling is affection.** A Socratic question means answer it. Answering wrong is fine; not engaging is not.
